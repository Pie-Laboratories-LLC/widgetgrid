# Funding and promotion: *The Last Naming* and the full game

Ideas from 2026-10-21, for further thought. Nothing here is decided. The
mini-game *The Last Naming* is free; its job is to draw people toward
funding a future game that will be sold.

## The problem to avoid

Twenty people play the mini-game, then nothing happens. Six months later a
desperate "hey, come support my thing" email goes to the two people who
signed up. Be aggressive about the pitch from launch day: catch interest
when it's highest, and never go quiet.

## Capture interest at launch

- **A list you own, separate from the blog subscription.** People who
  played the game signed up for the game, not for posts about EKS upgrades.
  (Site work: a list name on each subscription.)
- **Ask at the end of the mini-game.** The minute after it ends is the
  moment of most interest. Use an email field there, not a link off-site.
- **The landing page asks too**, under the live scene (see below).
- **A Kickstarter pre-launch page** ("Notify me on launch") up the day the
  mini-game launches, even if the campaign is months away. Follower count
  before launch is about the best predictor of whether a campaign funds,
  and Kickstarter favours projects with a strong first 48 hours.
- **A Steam "Coming Soon" page** ($100 per game) if the full game will sell
  on Steam. Wishlists are the number indie devs, publishers and Steam's
  algorithm all watch, and Steam emails every wishlister at launch. The end
  screen can offer both "follow the Kickstarter" and "wishlist on Steam".
- **Count it from day one:** starts, finishes, signups. Use a privacy-friendly
  counter (no cookies, no banner). These numbers show whether to fix the
  funnel or the traffic, and they belong in the pitch ("3,000 people played
  the prototype").

## Never go quiet

- The blog already works as a devlog. The rak, hair, crowds of a hundred
  faces, and the cat hauling a full-size charrette are exactly the posts
  that keep a list warm. One a month, sent to the game list, means the
  campaign announcement is the next post in a series people already open.
- The procedural work is the marketing: GIFs for r/proceduralgeneration,
  r/gamedev's Screenshot Saturday, and gamedev on Mastodon and Bluesky.
- Consider an itch.io page for the mini-game (an embedded browser build
  linking back here). itch has people who browse for small free games.

## Reach (there is no existing following)

That's true of almost every indie dev at the start. In rough order of cost:

1. **Content**: the devlog and GIFs above.
2. **Creator outreach.** Smaller channels (cozy games, Minecraft-adjacent,
   "weird indie games I found") happily cover a free, short browser game,
   because it's easy content. Keymailer and Lurkit help find them. One real
   playthrough from a 50k-subscriber channel can beat a large ad budget.
   The Hermitcraft members themselves are expensive to sponsor.
3. **YouTube ads.** Google Ads can target specific channels or videos
   ("placements"), so targeting Hermitcraft is possible.
   - Fit matters more than size: Hermitcraft viewers like building,
     community and gentle humour, and many are young.
   - Viewers can skip after 5 seconds, so open on the hook (the cat and the
     charrette), not a title card.
   - Test small, a few hundred dollars, and only once the funnel counts are
     live. Ads amplify a funnel that already works; on their own they rarely
     fund a campaign.

## Voice acting

Spend it where it's heard:

- **Trailer:** a narrator, or the lore keeper speaking a few lines. That
  costs a few hundred dollars for a short script and lifts both the trailer
  and the ads.
- **The whole mini-game** would cost much more and lock the script while the
  dialogue is still changing. Voice a handful of key lines at most; leave the
  rest as text for now.
- Casting Call Club suits indie budgets; Voices.com and Backstage reach more
  professional actors.

## The campaign

**Venue.** Kickstarter is still the main one for video games. BackerKit is
the newer alternative, but its strength is tabletop. Indiegogo lets you keep
the money even if you miss the goal, but brings much less of its own audience.

**The goal versus "leaving work" money.** $400k is the number that would make
walking away from a job comfortable, but it's too high as a first goal with
no following. Kickstarter is all or nothing: miss the goal and you get $0,
plus a public failure. So:

- Set the goal at the least you can deliver something real with. Anything
  above it is overfunding, which is normal. A $120k goal that ends at $210k
  is a success story; a $400k goal that reaches $210k pays nothing.
- Let the pre-launch follower count set the number. Rule of thumb: around a
  quarter of followers back, and the average game pledge is $30–40 once
  tiers exist. 3,000 followers means roughly a $25–40k campaign. $400k means
  10,000–13,000 backers, which needs 40,000 or more followers.
- $400k raised isn't $400k to live on. After Kickstarter and payment fees
  (around 8–10%), income tax and contractors (art, music, voice), think
  roughly $250k of runway. See an accountant before launch; the LLC helps.

**Tiers.** The tiers decide the average pledge more than the base price does.

| Pledge | Reward |
|---|---|
| $15 | The full game (the Minecraft-style early price) |
| $35 | The game, soundtrack, name in the credits |
| $40–50 | **Workshop edition**: the game plus the creation tools (see below) |
| $60 | Beta access |
| $150, limited | **Name a rak.** It's *The Last Naming*, and a herd counted by name is part of the story |
| $300+, very limited | A villager built from your face sliders and outfit, walking in the march |

The face sliders and seeded clothing already exist, so the top tiers are
cheap to deliver and mean a lot to the people who buy them.

**The pitch needs, at minimum:**

- what the full game is, in one sentence;
- what the money buys (time, art, music: be concrete);
- what backers get;
- that the mini-game is rough on purpose, and what finished looks like.

The roughness is a strength when it's presented as a working prototype
people can play today.

## Income beyond one campaign: the Minecraft route

Minecraft never used Kickstarter. It sold early access and kept selling it
while the game grew. A version of that:

1. **Patreon now**, before the campaign. Real recurring income, and it
   builds the habit and the audience.
2. **Kickstarter** at a goal you're confident of beating, maybe $80–150k
   depending on followers. It buys the first year and proves demand.
3. **Steam Early Access** once there's a playable slice. Ongoing sales are
   what replace a salary over time.

If the campaign makes $120k, that isn't money to leave a job for good, but
it's a year of runway with Patreon and Early Access ahead. Some people make
the leap in steps: a sabbatical or part-time on campaign money, then
quitting once Early Access sales show it's sustainable.

## Patreon tiers

- **$5/month:** the detailed devlog with plans, plus a monthly video on the
  month's progress. Make sure a video every month is sustainable.
- **$15/month: the creation tools.** Bâtiments, the atelier des créatures,
  maybe the animation studio, for people who like playing with sliders and
  JSON to make buildings. There's an audience that would rather build than
  play (Hermitcraft viewers watch other people build). Some will join,
  download and cancel; that's fine as long as each month brings something
  new (building styles, a creature, a tool).

## Selling the tools: open questions

1. **How people get them.** They're native C++ programs today, so selling
   them means:
   - Windows, macOS and probably Linux builds;
   - getting past macOS's and Windows's warnings for unsigned apps (Apple
     notarization costs $99 a year; Windows signing certificates cost more);
   - an installer and an update path.

   Later, the mini-game's browser export opens another route: a browser
   building workshop unlocked by a logged-in site account. Patreon's login
   API can confirm someone's tier. Desktop first; browser as a later bonus.
2. **Whether creations survive updates.** Once paying people have built
   houses in your Lua and JSON formats, changing those formats breaks their
   work. Either version the formats, or say up front that this is a workshop
   in progress and things will break. The second fits "rough on purpose";
   say it plainly.
3. **What people can do with what they make.** Let them share creations,
   because that's free promotion. Creations that end up in the game ("my
   house is in Oktul") are a huge draw, but they need contributor terms
   granting you the right to use them. Get a lawyer to look at that first.
4. **Support.** A "Workshop" section on the message board (a one-line seed
   change) lets buyers help each other.
5. **Language.** The tools are in French. Keep the names as flavour, but
   most buyers need the interface and documentation in English: a
   translation pass, or switchable languages?
6. **Order.** Building tools first: they're the most fun to play with and
   have the fewest moving parts (creatures need armatures; the animation
   studio needs clips). Each tool added later is another reason to stay
   subscribed. A standalone tools product could sell on itch.io, which
   handles creative tools well.

## A village mode, and videos

When the tools ship (bâtiments, atelier des créatures, Charronnerie...),
they need a mode that ties them together: **lay out a small village and
populate it.** Place buildings, set crowds of villagers walking with
seeded faces and clothes, put raks in the fields and wagons on the road.
The separate tools are for tinkerers; a village is something people show
off.

Make videos easy and explicitly welcome:

- **A written video policy**, linked from the tools and the site: "You may
  stream, record and monetize videos of the game and the tools." Many
  creators won't touch a game until they've seen that.
- **Make capture easy:** hide the interface, a free camera, slow flyovers
  or turntables of a village, maybe a built-in still or video export.
- **Villages people can share** as a single file, so a viewer can load the
  village they just watched being built.
- **Somewhere to show them:** a "Villages" section on the message board,
  and featuring the best ones in the monthly devlog, which credits creators
  and gives them a reason to keep making things.

## The landing page

A live scene of the Oktul valley with a couple of raks wandering and
grazing. It's the natural first slice of the browser export: one terrain
chunk, the rak as glTF with its walk, a grazing layer (head down, chewing,
the odd look around; it doesn't exist yet), and simple wandering (walk to a
spot, graze 10–40 seconds, sometimes move on).

- A fixed camera, or a very slow drift.
- A still frame rendered from C++ shows first, while the scene loads. It's
  also the fallback (no WebGL, reduced motion) and the image shown when the
  link is shared.
- Open: is the scene a full-bleed backdrop with the title, Play button and
  pitch over it, or framed above them? What time of day and weather?

## Website work this implies (phase II)

- Subscriptions per list (the game list separate from the blog).
- The end-of-game ask: email, plus Kickstarter and Steam links.
- The landing page: scene, pitch, links to the pre-launch page.
- Start, finish and signup counts.
- Later: Patreon sign-in to unlock tools; a Workshop board section.
