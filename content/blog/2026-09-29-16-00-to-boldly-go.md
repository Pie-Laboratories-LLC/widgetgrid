# To boldly go...

It might be the proudest announcement of my career that I have spent about nine
months building something that a person with a passing understanding of Blender
and a better understanding of Unity could knock off in a day or two:

[![To Boldly Go](./Boldly-Go.png)](https://youtu.be/vzdqFi0rQy0)

Regular readers of the blog, or those with the wherewithal to scroll down to
other articles, will recognise that I recently hit a milestone with Constructive
Solid Geometry, or CSG. The thing is, I had a demo of an armature skinner in
JavaScript. I created an armature, then procedurally generated splines that
represented cross-sections of the torso.

Here's where I was stuck: I could see how to procedurally generate the other
parts (arms, legs, etc.), but I didn't see how to glue them together. The idea
struck me that with a CSG library I could build the parts separately and then
combine them with CSG operations. Well and good; and honestly, you can set aside
the performance considerations, because these would be generated offline and
loaded at runtime.

![Tin Man](./Tin-Man.png)

Claude, in his inestimable wisdom (and wide spidering of GitHub), suggested a
better approach. One of the 3D primitives I created was a spinner, which took a
spline and rotated it around the y axis. It worked by generating points on the
spline and rotating them around that axis. The points are then stitched together
using a clever application of the Bresenham line-drawing algorithm.

Given this stitching work, the solution to joining different parts of the body
to the torso became, not light work, but a straightforward grind.

![Tin Man 2](./Tin-Man-2.png)

The thing that makes me happy about this result, the nine months in lieu of a
day's work, is that now I can automate the creation not just of humanoid shapes
like the ones marching in this scene, but of all creatures great and small, real
and fantastic. That's the payoff. And the CSG work is hardly lost: I can use it
to procedurally generate buildings, and with the aforementioned spinner and some
other primitives I can pretty well populate a relatively simple world.


I started this project in C# and, for various reasons (most notably the Vulkan
API, but also a latent love I bear for C++), switched to it. So those
nine months of effort have primarily shown themselves as page after page of text
on the console, tediously calculating angles and where points _should_ be. This
represents a true step forward in my passion project, and it's worth a little
celebration 🎉.

![Skin Man](./Skin-Man-2d.png)

There's still a long way to go to generate a better humanoid shape with proper
humanoid features. But for now I love the flat-headed tin-man look; hey, I've
worked for people who look like that.

The colours come from golden-ratio-generated pastels that were useful for
debugging the assignment of weights to the mesh. Each of the four characters has
its own tint blended in, giving each a different hue. They're nothing so much as
a veritable pride parade of success without vendors, and I am pleased as can be.
